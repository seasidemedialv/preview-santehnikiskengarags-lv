The other thing to know here is Riga’s rule on fixtures lower than the cover of the nearest sewer manhole. Most homes in the neighbourhood are historic, and their pipes may have been put in later than the walls themselves.

Pipes in historic houses around Kalna and Ludzas iela

In the geography section of the neighbourhoods portal, the residential areas lie mainly in the central part around Kalna, Ludzas and Latgales iela, where the building stock is mostly historic. Since May 2024 the neighbourhood has been called the Latgale neighbourhood; until then it was called Maskavas forštate. The history section says that mainly the wooden buildings put up in the suburb in the early 19th century have survived. In such houses water supply and sewerage may not have been planned from the start but added later, even in several stages.

Pipes may then run over floorboards, along walls in boxing or through partitions, and steel, cast iron, copper and plastic can meet within one flat. A timber structure moves over the years and floors are uneven, so in places a waste pipe may have lost its fall or even reversed it. Joints in a wall or under the floor may be out of sight. A leak can show far from where it starts, because water travels along the wood. At an accessible, visible spot, a leak can usually be sealed without opening the wall, but a hidden spot has to be found first.

Two kinds of ground in one neighbourhood

  • North-east on a reshaped dune mass

    Between the railway and Rēznas, Latgales and Lāčplēša iela, the portal places the neighbourhood outside the Daugava valley, on a reshaped mass of dunes. Water soaks well into sandy dune soil, but dry sand in a trench slides in easily. In an old pipe that has worked for a long time, sand can get in through loose sockets and settle. If sand has built up in a yard pipe, a snake can only shift it, while a pressurised water jet helps to wash it out, starting from the lower manhole.

  • South-east at 6–8 m above sea level

    Between the railway and Slāvu, Latgales and Rēznas iela the ground is 6–8 m above sea level, and the account calls building conditions there relatively favourable. For a yard pipe that means fewer surprises in the trench, but it says nothing about an old pipe’s condition or its fall to the manhole. If the drain runs slowly, jetting can wash deposits off the pipe walls. In both cases it matters to know exactly where the first manhole is and how deep the house pipe enters it, because everything else depends on that.

The slope and fixtures below the cover

At Latgales iela, formerly Maskavas iela, the neighbourhoods portal describes a marked slope: across a strip 80–150 m wide the ground rises by 5–7 m. For houses in this strip one side of the yard may be noticeably higher than the other. The yard pipe may run to the street side with a steep fall, or, the other way round, the house floor may be lower than the cover of the manhole the pipe enters. So before a new fixture goes into a cellar or ground floor, establish two separate things. One is where the nearest sewer manhole cover is, the other how high the new fixture’s waste will sit relative to it. If the fixture stays above the cover, it is connected in the ordinary way.

Riga City Council binding regulations No. 17 (15 December 2017), point 63.8 covers fixtures below the level of the nearest sewer manhole cover, such as a sink, toilet, floor drain or drainage. They may not be connected to the sewer without a special one-way valve or electric shut-off gate. Bear this in mind when putting a shower, washing machine or toilet in a cellar. Source: likumi.lv, checked 25 November 2026.

Board floors, timber walls and tight rooms

In a wooden house the waste pipe lies crooked on the floorboards. Can it be fixed without a new floor?

Usually yes. If the pipe runs above the floor, or below it for only a short stretch, it is disconnected, laid with the right fall and fixed to the wall or joists with brackets. If the fall was lost because the floor has settled, the pipe can be raised at the fixture end or lowered at the stack. A few boards are opened only where the pipe passes through the floor. Renewing the whole floor is rarely needed, only if the old pipe is built into it along its full length and the floor around it has rotted. Brackets go close enough that the pipe does not sag between them and hold water.

A tap in a timber wall is seeping. Must the panelling be opened beforehand?

Not straight away. First close the water and watch where damp appears: at the tap rosette, at the foot of the wall or on the floor by the riser. If drops are visible from outside at the tap supplies, resealing the joint is often enough. If the wood in the wall is wet further from the tap, the panelling is opened over a small area right at the pipe, not the whole wall. Damp wood must then be left to dry before the wall is closed. If the pipe in the wall is old steel, once it is open it may pay to replace a short length around the damaged spot as well.

The toilet room is narrow. Can the cistern be swapped for a slimmer one?

Yes, there are slim exposed cisterns and ones made for small rooms. Check that the new cistern fits the existing pan, since cistern and pan are often one set with its own fixing, and that the water supply is where it can reach the new cistern. If the cistern only fits another pan, both are replaced. In a very narrow room the alternative is a cistern in the wall on a hidden frame, but the wall must then be thick and stiff enough. It is worth choosing a mechanism whose spare parts are easy to buy.
