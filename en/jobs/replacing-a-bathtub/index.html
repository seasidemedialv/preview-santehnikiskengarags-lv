Bath replacement - cardboard under the tub for its first fill

A bath replacement is finished not when the tub is level but when it has been filled for the first time and stays dry underneath.

Levelling a new bath on legs or a frame and joining it to the waste with an overflow costs from €60. Buying the bath, cutting up and carrying out the old one, the tap and tiling are outside this price.

Cardboard underneath and the first fill

For its bath the maker Duravit suggests a simple check: leave a piece of cardboard under the tub, fill it with water and check for leaks. Cardboard soaks up even a few drops and shows where they fall, before the bath is covered by a panel or tiles.

The check makes sense right now, because after finishing the waste and overflow are hidden. A full bath also lets you test the overflow joint, which water does not reach every time in daily use.

The instructions of the US maker Kallista for an acrylic bath split the test into steps. Run cold and hot water and inspect the supply joints, let water out and inspect the waste joints, then fill to the overflow and look for leaks again. All this must be done before finishing. Kallista requires access to all end connections to be planned in from the start, for example through a removable section of panel.

Material and standard service life

Annex 2 of Cabinet Regulation No. 907 gives this average standard service life for baths:

Type of bath Average standard service life
Enamelled cast-iron bath 60 years
Enamelled metal bath 50 years
Polymer bath 30 years

Figures from Annex 2 of Cabinet Regulation No. 907 (viewed on likumi.lv on 25 November 2026). They are planning figures for normal conditions of use, not a date by which a bath must be replaced.

The material also changes the work. The price depends on the bath material and weight, and an old cast-iron bath usually has to be cut up before it can be carried out at all. Door and hallway widths decide whether the new bath gets into the flat in one piece.

Legs, frame and level

A new bath is levelled on adjustable legs or a frame. A frame matters for an acrylic bath, because without support its base can bend under a person’s weight, and then the joint at the wall cracks. A steel bath is stiffer, but it too must stand firmly on all its legs.

The rim along the wall is set level, yet water must run off the base to the waste without staying in the corners. The waste height also changes the price: if the old waste pipe is too high for the new bath, it has to be adjusted.

The same Kallista instructions require that the bath does not rest on its rim. The weight must lie on the levelling supports or the base, with a gap of about 2 mm between frame and rim underside. The bath is lifted only from below, not by the rim, and the floor must be even and able to bear the load given in the model’s documentation.

Silicone joint and wall finish

Duravit states: seal the bath around the edges with silicone, let it dry as the silicone maker directs and choose a water-resistant wall finish. The curing time depends on the particular silicone, so read it on the packaging instead of guessing.

The joint between bath and wall is where water strikes at every wash. Tiling and wall finishing are not part of the bath installation price.

Bath, joint and a shower instead

Must a 60-year-old cast-iron bath be thrown out?

No. The 60 years in the table of Cabinet Regulation No. 907 is an average for planning a building. If the enamel is sound and the waste does not leak, the bath may serve longer.

When may the new bath be used after the silicone joint?

Duravit directs that the silicone be left to dry following the silicone maker’s instructions. The time is written on the packaging of the particular silicone.

What changes if the shower will sit lower than the old bath waste?

Then the waste pipe may have to be lowered, or a higher tray chosen. It is one of the factors that decide how much pipework is needed.
